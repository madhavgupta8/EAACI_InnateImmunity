1. Neutrophils: numbers, life span, granules

An adult makes more than 1 × 1011 neutrophils per day. Each neutrophil circulates from a few hours to up to 5 days. Abbas p.14 G-CSF and GM-CSF stimulate neutrophil production. Abbas p.14 In tissues, a neutrophil lives 1–2 days. Tissue-resident macrophages can live for years. Abbas p.15

Neutrophils have two granule types. Abbas p.14

  • Specific granules: lysozyme, collagenase, elastase (Abbas; see the source conflict below).
  • Azurophilic granules: myeloperoxidase (MPO), defensins, cathelicidins.

Janeway states that neutrophil α-defensins are stored in "primary" granules, which fuse with phagosomes. Janeway p.46

Source conflict — granules

Abbas lists elastase in the specific granules and cathelicidins in the azurophilic granules. Abbas p.14 Janeway places neutrophil elastase in the azurophilic (primary) granules. Janeway p.534 Janeway also stores cathelicidin propeptides in the secondary (specific) granules. Elastase from the primary granules activates them. Janeway p.46 For an exam item on the granule location of elastase or cathelicidins, confirm the answer with the expert.

Dead and dying neutrophils are a major part of pus. Janeway p.82

Neutrophils are usually the most numerous leukocytes in infected tissue within 24–48 hours. Monocytes then replace them. Abbas p.50 Janeway states that neutrophil influx usually peaks within the first 6 hours. Janeway p.106

Exam pearl

Neutrophils make little or no nitric oxide (NO). Macrophages make NO after they induce iNOS. Abbas p.15 No pus at an infected site points to a neutrophil recruitment defect (see LAD below). Abbas p.488

2. Leukocyte extravasation

2.1 Adhesion molecules

Two classes of molecules attach leukocytes to endothelium: selectins and integrins. Selectins mediate the first, low-affinity adhesion in postcapillary venules. Abbas p.44

2.2 The four steps

  1. Rolling. Endothelial E- and P-selectin bind sialyl Lewis X on the leukocyte. The bonds have low affinity and a fast off-rate. They form and break, so the cell rolls. Abbas p.49
  2. Integrin activation. Endothelial heparan sulfate holds chemokines and shows them to the rolling cell. Abbas p.48 Chemokine receptor signals change the integrin shape to high affinity. This is "inside-out" signalling. Abbas p.46
  3. Firm arrest. Cytokines increase endothelial ICAM-1 and VCAM-1. ICAM-1 binds LFA-1 and MAC-1. VCAM-1 binds VLA-4. The cell stops and spreads. Abbas p.49
  4. Transmigration. The cell usually moves between endothelial cells (paracellular transmigration). Abbas p.49 CD31 takes part, and the VE-cadherin junction opens for a short time. Abbas p.50
Exam pearl — terminology

Abbas uses "diapedesis" for the passage between endothelial cells. Abbas p.49 Janeway uses "diapedesis" for the later passage through the basement membrane. Janeway p.106 Read the question carefully. The order of events is the same in both books, but the four steps are split differently. Janeway's four steps are rolling, tight integrin binding, crossing the endothelium and basement membrane, and migration through the tissue. Janeway p.106

Chemotaxis is cell movement along a concentration gradient toward the source of a chemoattractant. Abbas p.46 Chemokine receptors are seven-transmembrane GPCRs. Abbas p.47 CXC chemokines with an ELR motif attract neutrophils. Abbas p.46

4. Opsonins and phagocytosis

Opsonization is the coating of a pathogen with antibodies and/or complement proteins. Phagocytes then take it up more readily. Janeway p.49 Phagocytosis is an active, energy-dependent engulfment of particles larger than 0.5 µm. Abbas p.95

  1. Recognition. The microbe binds a phagocyte receptor. Some receptors are PRRs, for example C-type lectins and scavenger receptors. Others bind opsonins. Abbas p.95
  2. Engulfment. The membrane forms a cup around the particle. The cup closes and pinches off. Abbas p.95
  3. Phagosome. The new vesicle holds the particle and fuses with a lysosome. Abbas p.95
  4. Phagolysosome. Most microbicidal mechanisms are concentrated here. Abbas p.95
  5. Killing. TLR, Fc receptor, C3b receptor, IFN-γ receptor and CD40 signals activate killing. Abbas p.95

5. Killing mechanisms

Three classes of microbicidal molecules are the most important: ROS, NO and proteolytic enzymes. Abbas p.95–96

5.1 Respiratory burst

Phagocyte oxidase (NADPH oxidase) is a multisubunit enzyme. It assembles in activated phagocytes, mainly in the phagolysosome membrane. Abbas p.96 In resting neutrophils, the cytochrome b558 subunits (gp91 and p22) sit in secondary granule membranes. The p40, p47 and p67 subunits are in the cytosol. Janeway p.81 fMLP and C5a receptor signals activate Rac2, which the assembly needs. Janeway p.80

  1. NADPH oxidase reduces molecular oxygen (O2) to superoxide (O2•−). NADPH is the cofactor. Abbas p.96
  2. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) converts superoxide to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Abbas p.96 Janeway p.81
  3. MPO uses H2O2 to change halide ions into toxic hypohalous acids, for example hypochlorous acid (HOCl). Abbas p.96
  4. Other reactions also make the hydroxyl radical. Janeway p.81

The process uses oxygen, so it is called the respiratory burst. Abbas p.96 Potassium and hydrogen ions then enter the phagolysosome. This releases and activates granule enzymes such as cathepsin G and elastase. Janeway p.81 Janeway notes that ROS alone may not kill. ROS-driven K+ influx sets the vacuole pH for microbicidal proteins. Janeway p.536

5.2 Nitric oxide

iNOS is absent in resting macrophages. TLR signals induce it, especially together with IFN-γ. Abbas p.96 iNOS converts arginine to citrulline and releases NO. NO combines with H2O2 or superoxide to make peroxynitrite, which kills microbes. Abbas p.96

5.3 Enzymes and antimicrobial peptides

  • Proteases: elastase and cathepsin G are important for bacterial killing in neutrophils. Abbas p.96
  • Defensins: cationic peptides from epithelia and from neutrophils. They mainly disrupt microbial membranes. Abbas p.80
  • Lysozyme digests the cell wall of some Gram-positive bacteria. Lactoferrin holds iron away from microbes. Janeway p.80

5.4 Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)

Neutrophils can extrude their DNA and granule contents. The neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) is made of DNA and histone strands with bound lysozyme, elastase and defensins. NETs trap and kill bacteria and fungi. Abbas p.96 NET formation needs PAD4 (histone citrullination), elastase, MPO and phagocyte oxidase. Abbas p.96 The neutrophil usually dies (NETosis). Excess NET formation contributes to autoimmune and inflammatory diseases. Abbas p.96

Clinical link

NET formation needs phagocyte oxidase and MPO. Abbas p.96 So the respiratory burst is linked to both intracellular killing and NETs.

6. Phagocyte defects

Phagocyte defects cause invasive bacterial or fungal infections of the skin, liver and respiratory tract. The fungi are mainly Aspergillus and Candida. Abbas p.486 Janeway sorts the defects into four groups: production, adhesion, activation and killing. Janeway p.533

Analogy

Think of a fire brigade. Neutropenia: no fire fighters. LAD: the fire truck cannot stop at the burning house. CHS: the hoses do not connect to the water tank. CGD: the water tank is empty.

6.1 Chronic granulomatous disease (CGD)

  • Cause: mutations in components of NADPH oxidase. Abbas p.486
  • Genetics: about two-thirds are X-linked recessive. The X-linked gene encodes gp91, the 91-kD subunit of cytochrome b558. The rest are autosomal recessive. Abbas p.486 Autosomal forms can be milder, with later onset. Janeway p.82
  • Frequency: about 1 in 200,000 in the United States. Abbas p.486
  • Organisms: catalase-producing bacteria such as Staphylococcus. Catalase destroys the H2O2 that host cells could make from residual superoxide. Abbas p.487 Invasive Aspergillus infection is the leading cause of death. Abbas p.487
  • Granulomas: uncontrolled infection drives T cell-mediated macrophage activation. This forms granulomas and gives the disease its name. Abbas p.487
  • Complication: secondary HLH. Abbas p.498
  • Management: antibiotic and antifungal prophylaxis, IFN-γ, and consideration of HSCT; gene therapy trials are ongoing. Abbas p.487 After HSCT, neutrophil superoxide production of at least 15% of normal greatly improves resistance to infection. Abbas p.487
Check current guidance

Treatment statements come from Abbas 11th ed. Check them against current guidelines. The named diagnostic assays for CGD (dihydrorhodamine flow test, nitroblue tetrazolium test) are not covered in provided sources — needs expert input. The sources only state that CGD phagocytes make too little superoxide to kill ingested microbes. Abbas p.486–487

6.2 Myeloperoxidase (MPO) deficiency

MPO deficiency impairs intracellular killing. The phenotype is like CGD but less severe. Janeway p.536 Janeway lists G6PD deficiency, MPO deficiency and CGD together as defects of the respiratory burst. Janeway p.534

6.3 Leukocyte adhesion deficiencies (LAD)

All LADs are autosomal recessive. Neutrophils fail to reach the infection. Patients have severe bacterial infections from early life and cannot make pus. Abbas p.488 The infections resist antibiotic treatment, and wounds heal poorly. Janeway p.535

6.4 Chédiak–Higashi syndrome (CHS)

  • Autosomal recessive. Recurrent pyogenic infections, partial oculocutaneous albinism, giant lysosomes in neutrophils, monocytes and lymphocytes. Abbas p.488
  • Gene: LYST, which regulates lysosome trafficking. Abbas p.488
  • Phagosomes and lysosomes do not fuse properly. Phagocytes have enlarged granules and kill poorly. Janeway p.536
  • NK cell function is impaired. Leukocytes also have defective chemotaxis and phagocytosis. Abbas p.488
  • Metallic silver hair, poor vision, platelet dysfunction with bleeding. Janeway p.540
  • Without transplantation, HLH usually develops: fever, disseminated intravascular coagulation, organomegaly, hemophagocytosis. Abbas p.488

6.5 Congenital neutropenias

  • SCN (Kostmann disease): neutrophil count persistently below 0.5 × 109/L. Janeway p.533
  • Cyclic neutropenia: counts swing from near normal to very low, with a cycle of about 21 days. Janeway p.533
  • Genes: ELA2 (neutrophil elastase) is the most common cause of SCN. Some ELA2 mutations cause cyclic neutropenia. Janeway p.534 HAX1 deficiency gives an autosomal recessive SCN. Janeway p.534
  • Risk and treatment: higher risk of myelodysplasia or myeloid leukemia; G-CSF is the mainstay of treatment. Janeway p.534

6.6 Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD)

MSMD is severe disease from weakly virulent mycobacteria and other intracellular pathogens such as Salmonella. BCG vaccine can disseminate. Abbas p.498 IL-12 from dendritic cells and macrophages drives IFN-γ production by T cells and NK cells. Abbas p.498 Mutations hit IL-12p40, IL-12Rβ1, both IFN-γ receptor chains, STAT1 or NEMO (IKKγ). Abbas p.498 IL-12 and IL-23 share p40, so p40 deficiency removes both cytokines. Janeway p.538 Total absence of the IFN-γ receptor markedly reduces granuloma formation. Janeway p.538

Differential pearl

MyD88 or IRAK4 deficiency gives severe pyogenic infections with little inflammation ("cold" infections). Janeway p.535 This is a TLR signalling defect, not a phagocyte killing defect.

5 key points

  1. Extravasation has four steps: selectin rolling (sialyl Lewis X), chemokine-driven integrin activation, integrin–ICAM-1 arrest, transmigration. Abbas p.49
  2. CXCL8 (via CXCR1/2), C5a, LTB4 and formyl peptides attract neutrophils; CCL2 (via CCR2) attracts monocytes. Abbas p.50 Abbas p.48
  3. FcγRI binds IgG; CR1 binds C3b and C4b; CR3 (MAC-1) binds iC3b and is also an adhesion integrin. Abbas p.297–309
  4. NADPH oxidase → superoxide → (SOD) H2O2 → (MPO) hypohalous acid. CGD blocks the first step; about two-thirds are X-linked gp91. Abbas p.96 Abbas p.486
  5. LAD-1 = CD18, LAD-2 = no sialyl Lewis X, LAD-3 = KINDLIN-3 with bleeding; CHS = LYST with giant lysosomes and albinism. Abbas p.488

3 exam traps

  1. LAD-2 is a rolling defect (selectin ligand), not an integrin defect. LAD-1 and LAD-3 affect integrins: LAD-1 lacks the protein, LAD-3 cannot activate it. Abbas p.488
  2. MPO is not the enzyme that makes superoxide or H2O2. NADPH oxidase makes superoxide, SOD makes H2O2, MPO makes hypohalous acid. Abbas p.96
  3. In LAD, the blood neutrophil count is often high (leukocytosis), but tissue has no pus. Do not confuse this with neutropenia. Abbas p.488
