M1-A1 Type A Key: D

A 19-year-old man with cystic fibrosis has repeated lower-airway bacterial infections. Sputum cultures grow bacteria that colonize the airway surface. Blood cultures stay negative and there is no evidence of tissue invasion. Which failure of innate defense best explains this pattern?

  1. A Loss of tight junctions between airway epithelial cells
    Tight junctions block passage of microbes between cells. Their loss would favor invasion, but these bacteria do not cross the epithelium. Abbas p.80; Janeway p.44
  2. B Absent alpha-defensin production by Paneth cells
    Paneth cells sit in the crypts of the small bowel. They are not the airway defense that fails in cystic fibrosis. Abbas p.80
  3. C Deficient histatin secretion by the salivary glands
    Histatins come from the parotid, sublingual and submandibular glands and act mainly against fungi such as Candida. Janeway p.46
  4. D ✔ Impaired mucus clearance by ciliary action
    In cystic fibrosis, thick mucus or impaired ciliary movement causes lung infections by bacteria that colonize the epithelial surface but do not cross it. Janeway p.44; Abbas p.80
  5. E Lack of intraepithelial T lymphocytes with limited TCR diversity
    Intraepithelial T cells are a normal barrier component. Cystic fibrosis is the textbook example of failed mucus flow, not of absent intraepithelial lymphocytes. Abbas p.80; Janeway p.44

M1-A2 Type A Key: B

A 35-year-old man is admitted with community-acquired pneumonia. On day 2 his temperature is 39.2 °C, his blood neutrophil count is raised, and his serum C-reactive protein (CRP) is many times above the upper reference limit. Which mechanism best explains the raised CRP?

  1. A Plasma cells secrete CRP as an early, antigen-specific antibody
    CRP is an acute-phase protein from the liver. Acute-phase proteins have no structural diversity and are induced nonspecifically, unlike antibodies. Janeway p.110; Abbas p.97
  2. B ✔ Hepatocytes increase CRP synthesis in response to IL-6, IL-1 and TNF
    IL-6, IL-1 and TNF from phagocytes and DCs increase CRP synthesis by liver cells; plasma CRP can rise up to 1000-fold in infection. Abbas p.88; Abbas p.97
  3. C Neutrophils release preformed CRP stored in their granules
    The long pentraxin PTX3, not CRP, is stored in neutrophil granules. CRP is made by hepatocytes. Abbas p.87; Abbas p.88
  4. D Macrophage IL-10 drives hepatic CRP production
    IL-10 is a negative-feedback cytokine that inhibits macrophages and DCs. It does not drive the acute-phase response. Abbas p.101
  5. E The hypothalamus secretes CRP as part of the febrile response
    TNF and IL-1 act on the hypothalamus to cause fever. CRP comes from hepatocytes, not from the hypothalamus. Abbas p.96; Abbas p.97

M1-A3 Type A Key: E

A vaccine team tests a purified recombinant protein antigen in healthy volunteers. Alone, the protein induces only weak T cell–dependent antibody responses. When the team adds an aluminium salt, the responses become strong. What is the main reason for this effect?

  1. A Aluminium increases the diversity of B-cell receptor genes
    Receptor diversity comes from somatic recombination of gene segments in lymphocytes, not from adjuvants. Abbas p.4
  2. B Aluminium cross-links the T-cell receptor and so replaces signal 1
    Signal 1 is antigen recognition. Adjuvants act on the innate side and increase MHC molecules and costimulators on DCs. Abbas p.100; Abbas p.101
  3. C Aluminium blocks IL-10 negative feedback in macrophages
    IL-10 is a negative-feedback regulator of macrophages and DCs. The sources describe adjuvant action as innate stimulation, not IL-10 blockade. Abbas p.101
  4. D Aluminium induces memory in innate cells, so later responses are faster
    Innate memory is limited, and innate responses to repeated exposure are usually the same as the first. Abbas p.4
  5. E ✔ Aluminium activates innate responses that make DCs express costimulators and cytokines (signal 2)
    Aluminium salts may act by inflammasome activation. Adjuvants make DCs express more MHC, costimulators (signal 2) and cytokines, and migrate to lymph nodes. Abbas p.101

M1-K1 KPrim Key: + / − / + / −

Innate recognition and the epithelial barrier. Which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. A (+) Innate immune receptors are germline-encoded and distributed nonclonally: all cells of one lineage carry identical receptors.
    Correct. Innate receptors are germline-encoded and nonclonal; adaptive receptors are clonal. Abbas p.63; Abbas p.64
  2. B (−) Lysozyme is more effective against Gram-negative than Gram-positive bacteria because it cleaves lipopolysaccharide.
    Incorrect. Lysozyme cleaves peptidoglycan and is more effective against Gram-positive bacteria. In Gram-negative bacteria, LPS covers the peptidoglycan. Janeway p.45
  3. C (+) Paneth cells in the crypts of the small bowel are a major source of alpha-defensins.
    Correct. Paneth cells are a major producer of alpha-defensins. Abbas p.80
  4. D (−) Human neutrophils produce functional theta-defensins as their main antimicrobial peptide.
    Incorrect. The single human theta-defensin gene is inactivated by a mutation. Janeway p.46

M1-K2 KPrim Key: + / + / − / +

Systemic effects of innate cytokines. Which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. A (+) Endogenous pyrogens induce cyclooxygenase-2 to make prostaglandin E2, which acts on the hypothalamus; aspirin reduces fever by blocking prostaglandin synthesis.
    Correct. TNF, IL-1 and IL-6 induce COX-2 and PGE2; prostaglandin synthesis inhibitors such as aspirin reduce fever. Janeway p.109; Abbas p.97
  2. B (+) Acute-phase proteins have broad specificity for microbial patterns but no structural diversity.
    Correct. Acute-phase proteins have broad specificity for PAMPs and antibody-like functions, but no structural diversity; they are induced nonspecifically. Janeway p.109; Janeway p.110
  3. C (−) Anti-TNF antibodies have shown a clear survival benefit in clinical trials in patients with sepsis.
    Incorrect. Anti-TNF antibodies and soluble TNF receptors have not shown benefit in patients with sepsis. Abbas p.97
  4. D (+) TNF blockade can reactivate tuberculosis, which shows the role of TNF in keeping infection local.
    Correct. TNF blockade can reactivate tuberculosis in apparently well patients with previous infection. Janeway p.109; Abbas p.94

M2-A1 Type A Key: B

A 46-year-old woman develops generalised urticaria and hypotension within minutes of an intravenous antibiotic given at induction of anaesthesia. She has never received this drug before. Specific IgE to the drug is negative. Which mast cell receptor most likely mediated this IgE-independent reaction?

  1. A FcεRII (CD23)
    CD23 is a low-affinity IgE receptor whose biologic role is not known; it does not explain an IgE-independent drug reaction. Abbas p.467
  2. B ✔ MRGPRX2
    MRGPRX2 on skin mast cells binds several antibiotics and anaesthetic drugs and activates mast cells without IgE; it likely mediates some allergy-like drug reactions. Abbas p.470
  3. C FcγRIIB
    FcγRIIB is an inhibitory (ITIM) receptor that co-aggregates with FcεRI and inhibits mast cell signalling; it does not trigger degranulation. Abbas p.470
  4. D CD16 (FcγRIIIA)
    CD16 is the low-affinity IgG receptor on NK cells that mediates ADCC; it is not the drug receptor on mast cells. Abbas p.84
  5. E NKG2D
    NKG2D is an activating NK cell receptor for stress ligands (MIC-A, MIC-B, ULBP), not a mast cell drug receptor. Abbas p.83

M2-A2 Type A Key: D

A 52-year-old man has had three episodes of unexplained anaphylaxis. Between episodes his baseline serum tryptase stays raised. A bone marrow biopsy shows clusters of mast cells with abnormal morphology. A mutation in which gene is most likely?

  1. A GATA2
    GATA2 loss-of-function causes loss of NK cells, monocytes, DCs and B cells, not a clonal mast cell disorder. Abbas p.488
  2. B CD18 (β2 integrin β chain)
    CD18 mutations cause leukocyte adhesion deficiency type 1 with bacterial infections, not mast cell expansion. Abbas p.487
  3. C FcεRI α chain
    FcεRI is the high-affinity IgE receptor on mast cells and basophils; it is not the gene linked to systemic mastocytosis in the sources. Abbas p.466
  4. D ✔ c-KIT
    Systemic mastocytosis is a clonal mast cell disorder associated with c-KIT mutations; the mast cells release more mediators, including tryptase. Abbas p.470-471
  5. E LYST
    LYST mutations cause Chédiak-Higashi syndrome with defective cytotoxic granules, not systemic mastocytosis. Abbas p.488

M2-A3 Type A Key: A

In a laboratory assay, a tumour cell line that has lost all surface MHC class I is mixed with blood lymphocytes from a healthy donor who was never exposed to the tumour. CD56-positive, CD3-negative cells lyse the tumour cells within hours. No antibody is present. Which mechanism best explains the killing?

  1. A ✔ Loss of inhibitory signals from KIRs and CD94/NKG2A ('missing self')
    The cells are NK cells. Inhibitory KIRs and CD94/NKG2A recognise MHC-I; when MHC-I is lost, inhibition stops and activating signals trigger killing. Abbas p.85
  2. B T-cell receptor recognition of tumour peptide on MHC class I
    CD3-negative cells have no T-cell receptor, and the target has no MHC-I to present peptide. Abbas p.82
  3. C ADCC through CD16 binding to IgG on the tumour cells
    ADCC needs IgG1 or IgG3 bound to the target; no antibody is present. Abbas p.84
  4. D Engagement of the activating receptor CD94/NKG2A by HLA-E
    CD94/NKG2A is an inhibitory receptor; it binds HLA-E, which is an MHC-I molecule that the tumour has lost. Abbas p.85
  5. E Release of IL-12 that directly lyses the tumour cells
    IL-12 enhances NK cytotoxicity and IFN-γ secretion; it is a cytokine signal, not a killing mechanism. Abbas p.85

M2-A4 Type A Key: C

A 16-year-old girl has had recurrent severe herpesvirus infections and extensive warts since childhood. Her blood shows almost no NK cells, and her monocyte, dendritic cell and B-cell counts are also low. Her mother has a similar history. Which is the most likely diagnosis?

  1. A Chédiak-Higashi syndrome
    Chédiak-Higashi syndrome is autosomal recessive; NK cell function is impaired by abnormal granules, which does not fit a dominant pattern with low monocytes, DCs and B cells. Abbas p.488
  2. B CD16 (FcγRIIIA) deficiency
    CD16 deficiency is a functional NK cell deficiency (autosomal recessive); NK numbers and other lineages are not low. Abbas p.488
  3. C ✔ GATA2 deficiency
    Autosomal dominant GATA2 mutations cause loss of NK cells together with low monocytes, DCs and B cells; NK deficiency presents with herpesvirus and papillomavirus infections. Abbas p.488
  4. D Leukocyte adhesion deficiency type 1
    LAD-1 (CD18 mutation) causes bacterial and fungal infections from defective leukocyte adhesion, not loss of NK cells. Abbas p.487
  5. E Chronic granulomatous disease
    CGD is a defect of phagocyte oxidase with bacterial and fungal infections; it does not remove NK cells, DCs or B cells. Abbas p.487

M2-A5 Type A Key: E

A 27-year-old woman with allergic asthma has airway eosinophilia and mucus hypersecretion within hours of allergen exposure. Biopsy studies suggest that the first IL-5 and IL-13 come from tissue-resident cells that lack a T-cell receptor and that respond to IL-33, IL-25 and TSLP from the epithelium. Which cell is this?

  1. A ILC1
    ILC1s express T-BET and produce IFN-γ; they respond to IL-12, IL-15 and IL-18. Abbas p.81
  2. B ILC3
    ILC3s express RORγt and produce IL-17 and/or IL-22; they respond to IL-1 and IL-23. Abbas p.81
  3. C NK cell
    NK cells are cytotoxic and secrete IFN-γ, not IL-5 and IL-13. Abbas p.82
  4. D Th2 cell
    Th2 cells make IL-5 and IL-13 but carry a T-cell receptor and arrive later in the tissue than ILC2s. Abbas p.465
  5. E ✔ ILC2
    ILC2s (GATA3) lack a TCR, reside in tissue, respond to IL-25, IL-33 and TSLP, and make IL-5 and IL-13; they can start allergic inflammation before Th2 cells arrive. Abbas p.94

M2-A6 Type A Key: B

A 38-year-old man with severe allergic asthma produces thick sputum. Microscopy shows many eosinophils and Charcot-Leyden crystals. Which eosinophil protein forms these crystals?

  1. A Major basic protein
    Major basic protein is a cationic granule protein toxic to helminths, bacteria and host cells; it does not form Charcot-Leyden crystals. Abbas p.473
  2. B ✔ Galectin-10
    Eosinophils make large amounts of galectin-10, which aggregates into Charcot-Leyden crystals at sites of chronic type 2 inflammation such as asthmatic bronchi. Abbas p.473
  3. C Eosinophil peroxidase
    Eosinophil peroxidase makes hypochlorous or hypobromous acid; it is not the crystal-forming protein. Abbas p.473
  4. D Tryptase
    Tryptase is a mast cell protease and a marker of mast cell activation. Abbas p.471
  5. E Eosinophil cationic protein
    Eosinophil cationic protein is a toxic granule protein, but galectin-10 forms the crystals. Abbas p.473

M2-K1 KPrim Key: + / − / − / +

Mast cells, basophils and eosinophils in allergic disease. Which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. A (+) Basophils express high levels of FcεRI and can be triggered when antigen binds IgE on their surface.
    Correct. Basophils, like mast cells, have high FcεRI expression, bind IgE and are triggered by antigen binding to the IgE. Abbas p.465; Abbas p.466
  2. B (−) Eosinophils are efficiently activated by allergen cross-linking of IgE bound to their FcεRI.
    Incorrect. Eosinophil FcεRI is very low and lacks a signalling chain; there is no convincing evidence of activation through IgE. Abbas p.473
  3. C (−) Mature mast cells circulate in the blood and migrate to tissues during allergic reactions.
    Incorrect. Mature mast cells are normally not found in the circulation; progenitors mature in tissues. Abbas p.465
  4. D (+) Tryptase is present in all human mast cells, whereas chymase is present only in some mast cell subsets.
    Correct. Tryptase is in all human mast cells; subsets contain tryptase only or tryptase and chymase. Abbas p.465

M2-K2 KPrim Key: + / − / + / −

Dendritic cells and NK cells. Which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. A (+) Plasmacytoid dendritic cells express high levels of TLR7 and TLR9 and are the main producers of type I interferon.
    Correct. pDCs have high TLR7 and TLR9 and are the body's major producers of type I IFNs. Abbas p.23
  2. B (−) CD16 on NK cells binds IgE and mediates antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity.
    Incorrect. CD16 (FcγRIIIA) is a low-affinity receptor for IgG (IgG1 and IgG3), not IgE. Abbas p.84
  3. C (+) The inhibitory receptor CD94/NKG2A recognises HLA-E, which displays peptides from other MHC class I molecules.
    Correct. CD94/NKG2A binds HLA-E; HLA-E displays peptides derived from other MHC-I molecules. Abbas p.85
  4. D (−) Follicular dendritic cells are bone marrow-derived cells that present protein antigens to naive T cells.
    Incorrect. Follicular dendritic cells are stromal cells, not bone marrow-derived, and do not present protein antigens to T cells. Abbas p.22

M3-A1 Type A Key: C

A 2-year-old boy has his second episode of invasive pneumococcal infection (pneumonia with bacteraemia). His parents report that he hardly had a fever during either episode, and the local signs of inflammation were mild. He has had several common viral infections without complications. Which defect best explains this picture?

  1. A Loss-of-function mutation in TLR3
    TLR3 loss of function gives recurrent herpes simplex encephalitis, not invasive pyogenic infection. Abbas p.489
  2. B Mutation in the IL-12 receptor β1 chain
    IL-12Rβ1 mutations give susceptibility to Salmonella and mycobacteria, not pneumococcal 'cold' infection. Abbas p.93
  3. C ✔ Autosomal recessive IRAK4 deficiency
    MyD88/IRAK4 deficiency gives severe invasive bacterial (especially pneumococcal) infections early in life with little fever or inflammation; viral susceptibility is not increased. Abbas p.489; Janeway p.92, 535
  4. D Gain-of-function mutation in NLRP3
    NLRP3 gain of function (CAPS) gives excess IL-1 with fever and rash, the opposite of a fever-poor picture. Abbas p.74-75
  5. E Homozygous mutation in UNC93B
    UNC93B deficiency impairs endosomal TLRs and type I IFN and predisposes to herpes simplex encephalitis. Abbas p.489

M3-A2 Type A Key: E

A 6-year-old girl is admitted with her second episode of herpes simplex virus-1 encephalitis. She has no history of severe bacterial, fungal or other viral infections. Immunoglobulins and lymphocyte subsets are normal. A defect in which pathway is most likely?

  1. A MyD88–IRAK4 signalling to NF-κB
    MyD88/IRAK4 defects give invasive pyogenic bacterial infections; IRAK4 deficiency adds no viral susceptibility. Abbas p.489; Janeway p.92
  2. B TLR5 recognition of bacterial flagellin
    TLR5 is a surface receptor for flagellin of motile bacteria and has no role in herpes simplex encephalitis in the sources. Abbas p.67
  3. C NOD2 recognition of muramyl dipeptide
    NOD2 senses bacterial peptidoglycan (MDP); its variants relate to Crohn's disease and Blau syndrome. Abbas p.72
  4. D Lectin pathway initiation by MBL
    Low MBL increases risk of various infections mainly when other immune defects coexist; it does not cause isolated HSE. Abbas p.88
  5. E ✔ Endosomal TLR3–TRIF signalling to type I IFN
    Defects in TLR3, UNC93B, TRIF, TRAF3 or TBK1 impair type I IFN downstream of TLR3 and cause herpes simplex encephalitis without broad viral susceptibility. Abbas p.489

M3-A3 Type A Key: A

A 34-year-old man has had attacks since childhood of fever, urticarial rash, joint pain and conjunctivitis. The attacks start a few hours after cold exposure. His father has the same attacks. Which mechanism best explains his disease?

  1. A ✔ Gain-of-function NLRP3 mutation with excess inflammasome-generated IL-1β
    Dominant NLRP3 (cryopyrin) mutations cause CAPS; in FCAS cold exposure triggers fever, urticarial rash, joint pain and conjunctivitis. Abbas p.74; Janeway p.537
  2. B MEFV mutation with dysregulated pyrin inflammasome
    FMF (MEFV/pyrin) presents with fever, serositis and arthritis, without cold-induced urticarial rash. Abbas p.74-75
  3. C TNFRSF1A mutation affecting TNF receptor I
    TRAPS gives periodic fever, myalgia and rash; cold triggering is not a feature in the sources. Janeway p.536-537
  4. D Gain-of-function NOD2 mutation with excess NF-κB
    NOD2 gain of function causes Blau syndrome with spontaneous inflammation of joints, eyes and skin. Abbas p.72; Janeway p.93
  5. E Partial loss of mevalonate kinase activity
    HIDS gives fever from infancy with high serum IgD and lymphadenopathy. Janeway p.537

M3-A4 Type A Key: D

A 58-year-old man has a third attack of acute monoarthritis of the first toe. Joint aspirate shows monosodium urate crystals. He does not respond to conventional anti-inflammatory drugs, and the rheumatologist proposes IL-1 blockade. Which innate mechanism explains why IL-1 blockade is effective?

  1. A Urate crystals bind TLR4 and signal through MyD88 alone
    TLR4 recognizes LPS (with MD2) and heat shock proteins; the sources link urate crystals to the inflammasome, not to TLR4. Abbas p.67, 74
  2. B Cytosolic DNA from crystals activates cGAS–STING
    cGAS–STING senses cytosolic dsDNA and induces type I IFN, not IL-1β. Abbas p.76
  3. C NOD2 in Paneth cells recognizes urate and activates RIP2
    NOD2 recognizes muramyl dipeptide from bacterial peptidoglycan and activates NF-κB through RIP2. Abbas p.72
  4. D ✔ Phagocytosed crystals damage lysosomes and activate NLRP3–caspase-1
    Urate crystals damage lysosomes and activate the NLRP3 inflammasome; caspase-1 then generates active IL-1β. IL-1 antagonists treat severe resistant gout. Abbas p.72, 74
  5. E Cytosolic urate activates caspase-4 and caspase-5 directly
    The noncanonical caspase-4/5 pathway is activated by cytosolic LPS, not by urate. Abbas p.74

M3-A5 Type A Key: B

A 45-year-old woman with gram-negative urosepsis has a plasma CRP that rises steeply over 48 hours. Which group of cytokines, produced after TLR4 recognition of LPS, mainly drives this hepatic CRP synthesis?

  1. A IL-12 and IL-18
    IL-12 and IL-18 mainly drive IFN-γ production and NK cell function, not acute-phase protein synthesis. Abbas p.93-94
  2. B ✔ IL-6, IL-1 and TNF
    IL-6, IL-1 and TNF induce hepatocytes to make acute-phase proteins; CRP can rise up to 1000-fold. Abbas p.88, 97
  3. C IFN-α and IFN-β
    Type I IFNs induce an antiviral state and more MHC class I; they are not the drivers of CRP synthesis. Abbas p.90
  4. D IL-33 released as an alarmin
    IL-33 is an epithelial alarmin released from damaged cells; it is not the hepatic acute-phase signal. Abbas p.94
  5. E CXCL8 and CCL2
    These chemokines recruit neutrophils and monocytes; they do not induce hepatic acute-phase proteins. Abbas p.94

M3-A6 Type A Key: E

A 5-month-old girl has recurrent fever, skin lesions due to cutaneous vasculopathy, and tachypnoea. Chest CT shows interstitial lung disease. Infection screening is negative. Which mechanism is most likely?

  1. A Gain-of-function NLRP3 with excess IL-1β
    CAPS gives fever, neutrophilic rash, bony lesions and CNS inflammation, not vasculopathy with interstitial lung disease. Abbas p.75

  5. E ✔ Gain-of-function STING with excess type I IFN
    STING gain of function causes SAVI, an interferonopathy with cutaneous vasculopathy, fever and interstitial lung disease. Abbas p.75, 77

Toll-like receptors. Which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. A (+) TLR9 sits in endosomes and recognizes unmethylated CpG DNA.
    TLR9 is endosomal and binds unmethylated CpG motifs, common in prokaryotic DNA but rare in vertebrate DNA. Abbas p.67, 69
  2. B (+) For LPS recognition, the accessory protein MD2 binds lipid A and the complex engages TLR4.
    MD2 binds the lipid A component of LPS and the complex interacts with TLR4; CD14 is also needed for efficient signalling. Abbas p.69
  3. C (−) TLR5 sits in endosomes and recognizes viral single-stranded RNA.
    TLR5 is a plasma membrane TLR that binds bacterial flagellin; TLR7 and TLR8 bind ssRNA. Abbas p.67, 69
  4. D (−) TLR3 depends on MyD88 to induce IFN-β.
    TLR3 is the only TLR that does not use MyD88; it signals only through TRIF. Abbas p.69

M3-K2 KPrim Key: + / − / + / +

NOD-like receptors and inflammasomes. Which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. A (+) Caspase-1 cleaves gasdermin D, and the N-terminal fragment forms pores in the plasma membrane.
    The gasdermin D N-terminal fragment polymerizes into pores through which IL-1β leaves the cell. Abbas p.72
  2. B (−) Neutrophils are the main cell type that dies by pyroptosis.
    Pyroptosis affects macrophages and dendritic cells, not neutrophils. Abbas p.74
  3. C (+) NOD2 recognizes muramyl dipeptide, and some NOD2 polymorphisms increase the risk of Crohn's disease.
    NOD2 senses MDP from gram-negative and gram-positive peptidoglycan; some variants raise Crohn's risk. Abbas p.72
  4. D (+) In humans, cytosolic LPS can activate caspase-4 or caspase-5 and cause pyroptosis.
    This is the noncanonical inflammasome pathway (caspase-11 in rodents). Abbas p.74

Soluble PRRs and cytosolic nucleic-acid sensors. Which of the following statements is/are correct?

  1. A (+) CRP binds phosphorylcholine and activates the classical complement pathway by binding C1q.
    CRP binds phosphorylcholine; CRP, SAP and PTX3 activate the classical pathway through C1q. Abbas p.87-88
  2. B (−) Ficolins recognize carbohydrates through a C-type lectin domain, like MBL.
    Ficolins have a fibrinogen-type recognition domain instead of a C-type lectin domain. Abbas p.89
  3. C (+) RIG-I recognizes viral RNA with a 5′-triphosphate, which capped host mRNA lacks.

M4-A1 Type A Key: C

An 8-month-old boy has recurrent bacterial skin infections and a wound that heals poorly. The infected sites show no pus. His blood leukocyte count is high. Flow cytometry shows very low CD11a, CD11b and CD11c on his neutrophils. Which gene is most likely mutated?

  1. A GDP-fucose transporter
    This causes LAD-2: sialyl Lewis X is absent, but β2 integrin (CD11/CD18) expression is not the primary defect. Abbas p.488
  2. B KINDLIN-3
    This causes LAD-3: integrins are present but cannot be activated by inside-out signalling. Abbas p.488
  3. C ✔ CD18 (β2 integrin chain)
    LAD-1 is caused by CD18 mutations, with absent or reduced β2 integrins (LFA-1, MAC-1, p150,95). Leukocytosis, poor wound healing and no pus fit. Abbas p.488
  4. D LYST
    LYST mutations cause Chédiak–Higashi syndrome, with giant lysosomes and partial albinism, not loss of CD11/CD18. Abbas p.488
  5. E gp91 subunit of cytochrome b558
    This causes X-linked CGD, a killing defect. Neutrophils reach tissue and integrin expression is normal. Abbas p.486

M4-A2 Type A Key: E

A 2-year-old girl has recurrent bacterial infections and a high blood leukocyte count. She also has severe mucosal bleeding that needed blood transfusions. Her platelet count is normal. Her skin, hair and eye pigmentation are normal. Neutrophil CD18 expression by flow cytometry is normal. Which mechanism best explains her disease?

  1. A Absent expression of β2 integrins on leukocytes
    CD18 expression is normal, so LAD-1 is unlikely. LAD-1 also does not cause a bleeding disorder. Abbas p.488
  2. B Absence of sialyl Lewis X on leukocytes
    LAD-2 blocks rolling and causes developmental abnormalities, not a platelet aggregation defect. Abbas p.488
  3. C Failure of NADPH oxidase to make superoxide
    CGD is a killing defect with granulomas and Aspergillus infections; it does not cause bleeding or block adhesion. Abbas p.486-487
  4. D Defective intracellular trafficking of lysosomes
    This is CHS (LYST). CHS can cause bleeding from platelet dysfunction, but it also gives partial albinism, silver hair and giant lysosomes. Her normal pigmentation, leukocytosis and normal CD18 favour LAD-3. Abbas p.488; Janeway p.540
  5. E ✔ Defective inside-out activation of integrins
    LAD-3 (KINDLIN-3) blocks chemokine-induced integrin activation in leukocytes and platelets. Platelet counts are normal but aggregation fails, so bleeding is severe. Abbas p.488

M4-A3 Type A Key: A

A 4-year-old boy has a Staphylococcus aureus liver abscess. Last year he had invasive pulmonary aspergillosis. A lymph node biopsy shows granulomas. His maternal uncle died in childhood of a severe infection. Which molecular defect is most likely?

  1. A ✔ Mutation in the gp91 subunit of cytochrome b558
    About two-thirds of CGD is X-linked recessive, due to gp91 mutations. Catalase-producing Staphylococcus, Aspergillus and granulomas are typical; the maternal-uncle history fits X-linked inheritance. Abbas p.486-487
  2. B Mutation in the p47 cytosolic oxidase subunit
    Mutations in other oxidase subunits give autosomal recessive CGD, often milder with later onset. The maternal-uncle history favours the X-linked form. Janeway p.82
  3. C Myeloperoxidase deficiency
    MPO deficiency gives a CGD-like but less severe phenotype; it does not fit X-linked inheritance with life-threatening infections. Janeway p.536
  4. D Mutation in the CD18 gene
    LAD-1 is autosomal recessive and causes absent pus and leukocytosis, not granulomas with intact recruitment. Abbas p.488
  5. E Mutation in IFN-γ receptor 1
    IFN-γ receptor defects cause MSMD, with mycobacterial and Salmonella infections rather than Staphylococcus and Aspergillus. Abbas p.498

M4-A4 Type A Key: B

A 35-year-old woman has occasional bacterial infections that respond well to treatment. Laboratory studies show that her neutrophils produce normal amounts of superoxide and hydrogen peroxide. However, her neutrophils cannot convert hydrogen peroxide into hypochlorous acid. Which enzyme is deficient?

  1. A Superoxide dismutase
    SOD converts superoxide to hydrogen peroxide; her H2O2 production is normal. Abbas p.96
  2. B ✔ Myeloperoxidase
    MPO uses H2O2 to convert halide ions into hypohalous acids. MPO deficiency gives a CGD-like but milder phenotype. Abbas p.96; Janeway p.536
  3. C NADPH oxidase (gp91 subunit)

    Credits: -2: 0, -1: 0, 0: 0, +1: 0.5, +2: 1
    Catalase-producing organisms such as Staphylococcus are troublesome in CGD, and invasive Aspergillus is the leading cause of death in CGD. Phagocyte defects typically involve skin, liver and respiratory tract. Abbas p.486-487
  2. Hypothesis: Terminal complement (C5-C9) deficiency · New info: He has had two episodes of disseminated Neisseria meningitidis infection.
    Credits: -2: 0, -1: 0, 0: 0, +1: 0.6, +2: 1
    In C5-C9 deficiency the only consistent clinical problem is disseminated Neisseria infection, because MAC lysis is important against these thin-walled bacteria. Properdin and factor D defects give a similar picture, so +1 earns partial credit. Abbas p.315
  3. Hypothesis: Leukocyte adhesion deficiency type 1 (LAD-1) · New info: The skin abscesses contained abundant pus.
    Credits: -2: 1, -1: 0.6, 0: 0, +1: 0, +2: 0
    LAD causes failure of neutrophil recruitment and inability to make pus; infections show no pus. Abundant pus argues strongly against LAD-1. Abbas p.488

A 9-year-old girl has had recurrent episodes of fever with an urticaria-like rash, joint pain and red eyes since early childhood. Episodes often follow exposure to cold. Her mother had similar episodes as a child. (Provisional panel credits.)

  1. Hypothesis: Cryopyrin-associated periodic syndrome (CAPS, NLRP3 gain of function) · New info: Her mother has progressive hearing loss.
    Credits: -2: 0, -1: 0, 0: 0.2, +1: 0.6, +2: 1
    Muckle-Wells syndrome and FCAS are dominantly inherited NLRP3 disorders with fever, urticarial rash, joint pain and conjunctivitis; Muckle-Wells also causes progressive deafness. Janeway p.537
  2. Hypothesis: Familial Mediterranean fever (FMF) · New info: Genetic testing finds a gain-of-function NLRP3 mutation.
    Credits: -2: 0.7, -1: 1, 0: 0, +1: 0, +2: 0
    FMF is caused by MEFV (pyrin) mutations, whereas CAPS is caused by gain-of-function NLRP3 mutations. A disease-causing NLRP3 mutation explains the picture and makes FMF much less likely. Abbas p.74-75
  3. Hypothesis: CAPS (NLRP3 gain of function) · New info: The attacks respond dramatically to anakinra, an IL-1 receptor antagonist.
    Credits: -2: 0, -1: 0, 0: 0.3, +1: 1, +2: 0.5
    CAPS is driven by excess IL-1 and responds to IL-1 antagonists such as anakinra. A response supports CAPS but is not specific, because IL-1 blockade also helps other IL-1-driven conditions such as severe gout. Abbas p.74; Janeway p.537
